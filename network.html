1.3 — Computer Networks, Connections and Protocols

Notebook Page • Detailed Master Revision • Exam Focus

Comprehensive study notes explaining how computers communicate, the physical and logical architecture of digital networks, hardware devices, and the suite of protocols governing data transfer across the globe.

1.3.1 — Types of Network

Networks are classified primarily by their geographical scale, scope, and who owns the physical infrastructure:

Network Type Geographical Scope & Ownership Detailed Explanation & Examples
PAN (Personal Area Network) Very small scale (within ~10 meters around a single individual). Owned by the user. Centers around a single person. Examples include connecting a smartphone to wireless Bluetooth headphones, a smartwatch syncing health data, or a wireless mouse connected to a laptop.
LAN (Local Area Network) Small geographical area — e.g., a single room, building, school campus, or office block. Owned entirely by the organization. Allows high-speed data sharing and peripheral sharing (e.g., printers). Connected using copper Ethernet cables (wired) or Wi-Fi (wireless access points). High data transfer speeds and strong security control.
WAN (Wide Area Network) Large geographical area spanning towns, cities, countries, or globally. Infrastructure is often leased from telecommunication companies. Because organizations cannot practically run private cables across continents, they lease lines (fiber optics, satellite links, telephone lines) from telecom providers. The Internet is universally recognized as the world's largest WAN.

1.3.2 — Network Topologies

A network topology defines how physical devices (nodes) and communication links are arranged relative to each other.

Feature Star Topology Mesh Topology
Structural Layout All devices (nodes) are individually connected to a central hub or switch. There is no direct link between standard workstations. Every device is connected directly to every other device (Full Mesh) or connected to multiple neighboring devices (Partial Mesh).
Key Advantages
  • Extremely reliable: If one cable or device fails, only that device is disconnected.
  • Easy to add/remove new devices without disrupting the network.
  • High performance with minimal data collisions.
  • Incredible fault tolerance: Multiple alternate paths exist for data packets. No single point of failure.
  • Very secure (data passing through dedicated lines cannot easily be intercepted).
Key Disadvantages
  • If the central switch or hub fails, the entire network goes down.
  • Requires a significant amount of cable as every device needs its own dedicated wire back to the switch.
  • Extremely expensive to install due to the massive quantities of cabling and specialized hardware ports required.
  • Complex maintenance, setup, and management.
Typical Use Cases School IT suites, modern office buildings, and residential home networks. The Internet backbone (routing between major ISP nodes), military command systems, and critical industrial infrastructure.

1.3.3 — Network Hardware

To establish communication between computers, specialized hardware components act as intermediaries and signal conditioners:

  • Router: Connects distinct, separate networks together (most commonly connecting a local LAN to the global WAN/Internet). Routers examine the destination IP address of incoming data packets and calculate the most efficient path forward.
  • Switch: Operates strictly within a single LAN to connect internal devices together. Unlike older "hubs" (which broadcast incoming data to every single port indiscriminately), an intelligent switch records device MAC addresses and forwards data only to the specific destination port intended, reducing network traffic and boosting security.
  • WAP (Wireless Access Point): A hardware transceiver that bridges wireless Wi-Fi devices (smartphones, tablets, laptops) onto a traditional wired Ethernet network infrastructure.
  • NIC (Network Interface Card): A physical circuit board or built-in chip inside a computer or device that grants network connectivity. Every single NIC in the world is assigned a unique, hardcoded physical address known as a MAC (Media Access Control) address during manufacturing.
  • Transmission Media: The physical channels carrying data signals:
    • Copper Twisted-Pair Cables (Ethernet/Cat5e/Cat6): Uses electrical signals; cost-effective over short distances within buildings.
    • Fibre Optic Cables: Uses pulses of light total internal reflection; immune to electromagnetic interference, capable of immense bandwidth over long trans-oceanic distances.
    • Wireless Radio Waves: Highly flexible and mobile, though susceptible to physical obstruction and interference.

1.3.4 — Client-Server vs. Peer-to-Peer Networks

Attribute Client-Server Architecture Peer-to-Peer (P2P) Architecture
Core Role Distribution Roles are clearly separated: A powerful dedicated server manages centralized files, security, and databases, while client workstations make requests to it. All devices (peers) hold equal status. There is no central server; every device can act as both a client (requesting files) and a server (sharing files).
Administration & Security Centralized control. User accounts, passwords, software updates, and backups are managed effortlessly from one central server location. Decentralized. Each device must be configured, secured, backed up, and updated individually by its respective user. Higher security risk.
Scalability & Performance Can handle heavy concurrent workloads smoothly, provided the central server has robust processing power and high bandwidth capacity. Performance can degrade as more peers join or leave the network, because processing load is distributed across consumer hardware.
Real-World Examples School networks, corporate office networks, web application hosting servers (like cloud storage or online banking). BitTorrent file sharing networks, direct printer sharing between two computers at home.

1.3.5 — Factors Affecting Network Performance

Network speed and user experience fluctuate based on several technical bottlenecks:

  • Bandwidth The theoretical maximum volume of data that can be transferred across a transmission medium per second (measured in Mbps or Gbps). Think of this as the width of a highway.
  • Latency The actual time delay (measured in milliseconds) it takes for a data packet to travel from the source sender to the destination receiver. Low latency is critical for real-time applications like online gaming and video calls.
  • Contention Ratio The ratio of users sharing the same bandwidth capacity at any given time. A low contention ratio (e.g., 1:1 dedicated business lines) ensures high speed, whereas high contention ratios (e.g., 50:1 residential broadband) slow down during peak evening hours.
  • Interference Physical walls, thick concrete, microwave ovens, and competing Wi-Fi channels can distort wireless radio signals, leading to packet loss and retransmissions.

1.3.6 — The Internet, Protocols, and Packet Switching

A protocol is a standardized set of rules that govern how data is formatted, transmitted, received, and processed across a network so different hardware manufacturers can understand each other.

The Crucial Distinction: Internet vs. WWW

Students often confuse these terms in exams:

  • The Internet: The physical global hardware infrastructure—cables, routers, servers, and switches connecting computers worldwide.
  • The World Wide Web (WWW): A service that runs on top of the Internet. It consists of interconnected hypertext documents and web pages accessed via browsers using HTTP/HTTPS.

Key Networking Protocols Suite

Protocol Primary Function & Detailed Role
TCP/IP
(Transmission Control Protocol / Internet Protocol)		 The foundational 2-layer engine of the internet. IP handles addressing and routing packets across multiple networks. TCP establishes connection sessions, breaks data down, numbers packets sequentially, checks for errors, and requests retransmission of lost packets.
HTTP / HTTPS
(HyperText Transfer Protocol [Secure])		 Governs the transfer of web pages between a web server and a client browser. HTTPS adds a layer of encryption (using SSL/TLS certificates) to protect sensitive data like credit card numbers from eavesdropping.
DNS
(Domain Name System)		 Acts as the "phone book" of the Internet. Automatically translates easy-to-remember human domain names (e.g., www.google.com) into numerical IP addresses (e.g., 142.250.190.46).
SMTP
(Simple Mail Transfer Protocol)		 Used specifically for pushing and sending outgoing emails from an email client to a mail server, or between mail servers.
IMAP
(Internet Message Access Protocol)		 Used for retrieving and syncing incoming emails from a mail server. Crucially, IMAP leaves a copy of the email on the server so you can access your inbox across multiple devices simultaneously.
FTP
(File Transfer Protocol)		 Used for uploading and downloading raw files securely between computers over a network connection.
📌 Deep Dive: The Lifecycle of Packet Switching

Instead of sending a massive file in one continuous stream (which would block network lines), the internet uses packet switching. Messages are broken down into small, manageable chunks called packets.

Each packet contains vital metadata:

  • Sender & Recipient IP Addresses: Ensures routers know where the packet originated and where it must ultimately go.
  • Sequence Number: Tells the destination computer the correct order to piece the chunks back together.
  • Data Payload: The actual fragment of the file or message being transmitted.

Exam Note: Because routers dynamically choose the best route moment-by-moment, packets often take entirely different physical paths and arrive out of order at the destination. TCP sorts and reassembles them, requesting replacements for any corrupted or dropped packets.
